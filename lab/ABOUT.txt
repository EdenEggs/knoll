  KNOLL — THE COMING-SOON PAGE AND THE LAB
  a plain-text tour of what this thing is and everything in it
════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

  This file covers two separate pieces that live in the same folder:

    site/index.html   the public coming-soon page for knoll.space
    site/lab/         a private workbench where the features get built

  Written for whoever opens this in six months and needs to remember how
  any of it works. Nothing here is a spec — it describes what is actually
  built and running today.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  1 · WHAT KNOLL IS
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Knoll is a shared space for internet communities — a fan page that the fans
themselves build, rather than one that a single admin maintains and everyone
else comments on.

The one-line pitch on the page:

    "Every gnome needs a Knoll."
    Knoll is a shared canvas where your community draws and builds
    together in real time.

The central metaphor is a hill (a knoll). A community gets a hill. Members
draw on it, stamp things onto it, leave notes, and vote on what stays. What
the community approves stops being a poll result and becomes part of the
place — a pond gets dug, a landmark gets named, an article gets written into
the law book. The gimmick that holds it together is that group decisions have
physical consequences on a shared piece of ground, and those consequences are
permanent.

Three claims the page makes:

    The fan page, by the fans
        Doodle, stamp shapes, leave notes, queue up the jukebox, or beat
        someone's snake high score. The best of it gets voted onto the page
        for good.

    Built by consensus
        Submit a design, let the community vote. What the hill approves
        becomes part of the page — forever.

    Your hill, your rules
        Pin the good stuff, tidy the mess, and keep the vibe yours with
        simple mod tools.

The tone throughout is deliberately silly-but-earnest: gnomes, toadstools,
lanterns, a mayor with no powers, a pond named Greg. The mechanics
underneath are real; the framing is a joke that everyone is in on.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  2.1  The hero: a wall you can actually draw on
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The top of the page is not a screenshot. It is a live canvas — the product,
running, with the marketing copy sitting on top of it. Everything you make is
saved to your own browser and is still there when you come back.

  THE TOOL DOCK (bottom centre, dark pill)

    Move        drag anything already on the wall
    Draw        freehand ink
                  · 4 pen widths (2.5 / 4.5 / 8 / 14)
    Sticker     stamp a shape
                  · 5 stamps: pointy hat, gnome, toadstool, big beard,
                    lantern
                  · 3 opacities (100% / 55% / 25%)
    Text        leave a note
                  · 5 fonts: Sans, Display, Serif, Mono, Marker

    5 colours   ink, pink, blue, green, orange
                  (a second, darker palette swaps in after dark so nothing
                   glows off the night sky)
    Undo        step back one piece
    Clear       wipe your wall

  A second row of option buttons appears above the dock whenever the chosen
  tool has options (widths, stamps, fonts). It hides itself in Move mode.

  PERSISTENCE
    Everything lives in localStorage under the key 'wallspace-wall-v4'.
    Strokes, stickers, notes and their positions all come back on reload.
    Nothing is uploaded. There is no account and no server for the wall.

  RESPONSIVE BEHAVIOUR
    Below ~760px the page enters "mini" mode: the music deck folds up, the
    playlist hides, the roaming props are re-placed so they stop covering the
    headline, and the tool dock collapses to a single row. Crossing the
    breakpoint re-lays the whole top band rather than just scaling it.

  2.2  The things living on the hero
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The wall is populated so it never looks empty. All of this is fake — there
are no other users yet — but it is all animated in real time, not looped
video.

  SIX GHOST CURSORS
    Named peers wandering the wall with colour-coded trails:
    mossy, pixelpete, juno~, opal, zip, vinny.

    An oversized gnome head that tracks your cursor with its eyes and blinks.
    It reacts to the signup form in two stages — eager when you touch the
    email field or the interest dropdown, full celebration once you actually

  THE SPORTS CAR
    A gnome tearing around a strip of the hero in a tiny car, tilting into
    corners. On narrow screens its lane moves so it stops cutting through the

  THE LOUNGE
    A parasol with a lantern strung off the canopy, and an attendant.

  THE LAWN GNOME
    Stands on the hill. Can be picked up and moved. In the sketches further

  DAY AND NIGHT
    The page reads your own clock and dresses itself accordingly.
      dawn  05:00 → 07:30      dusk  18:30 → 21:00
    A single 0..1 "night" dial drives the sky wash, the stars and the lantern
    flames, ramping smoothly. Anything where legibility matters — the copy,
    the ink already on the wall — uses a hard switch instead (at 26% night),
    because cross-fading text and background passes through a moment where
    the two match and the words disappear.
      Add ?hour=21 to the URL to force a time of day and inspect the other
      half without waiting for evening.

  THE PAPER
    Under everything is a dot grid, and under that, faint contour lines — a
    seamless 720px tile of four nested wobbly hills, generated the same way
    every time,
    so it is identical on every load. The same geometry feeds three surfaces:
    the hero by day (via CSS), the night sky (painted in, so the lines fade
    with the wash instead of vanishing), and the white section below the fold.

  3 · THE LAB  (site/lab/)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  What it is ..... a workbench for things that aren't ready yet. Every
                   sketch from the coming-soon page, built for real and
                   playable, so the idea can be judged by using it rather
                   than by looking at a drawing of it.
  Not deployed ... lab/ is untracked in git, so it does not ship with the
                   site. It is a local tool. Open lab/index.html (or serve
                   the site folder) to use it.
  Storage ........ everything is localStorage, per device, prefixed
                   'knoll-lab:'. No server, no accounts, nothing leaves the
                   machine.
  Shape .......... one HTML page, three CSS files (lab.css draws it, themes.css
                   dresses it, sticker.css cuts the newer machines out of a
                   different kind of paper), one small framework (lab.js), a
                   genre-skin switcher (theme.js), a tool dock that draws on
                   the bench (wall.js), shared modules, and sixteen
                   independent gizmos.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  3.1  The workbench itself  (lab.js + lab.css)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The lab is an infinite canvas with fourteen machines sitting on it. Each machine
("gizmo") is a self-contained panel with a dark handle bar, a name plate and
a serial number, that you can drag anywhere.

  THE CAMERA — the canvas moves like Figma's

    The page itself never scrolls. #bench is a fixed viewport and
    #bench-world is the frame everything hangs in; a camera (pan x/y + zoom)
    slides and scales that frame underneath you.

    There is no sheet. The world used to be painted — a page of dot-grid
    paper with an edge and a drop shadow — which read as one big box sitting
    on the bench, and gave the canvas a boundary it does not really have. The
    world is now invisible, and the bench's own grid, showing through it, IS
    the paper.

      hold the SCROLL WHEEL and drag ..... pan, from anywhere, even over a
                                           gizmo
      SPACE (or press H) and drag ........ pan — the hand tool; V or Esc
                                           goes back to the pointer
      drag the bare paper ................ pan (a small twitch is still a
                                           click, so buttons keep working)
      scroll / two-finger swipe .......... pan up and down
      SHIFT + scroll ..................... pan sideways
      CTRL/⌘ + scroll, or pinch .......... zoom about the pointer
      + / − .............................. zoom in and out
      0 or SHIFT+0 ....................... back to 100%
      SHIFT+1 or HOME .................... fit everything on screen
      ⤢ − 100% + ? ....................... the dock, bottom right; the ?
                                           opens a card with all of this

    Zoom runs from 2% to 400%. A list inside a gizmo keeps the wheel while it
    still has somewhere to scroll, and hands it back to the canvas when it
    hits the end. Dragging a gizmo to the edge of the screen pans the camera
    along with it. The camera position and zoom are remembered between
    visits.

    On touch: one finger drags to pan anywhere that isn't a control, a
    handle or a scrollable list. Pinch is the browser's own zoom.

    THE GRID RIDES THE CAMERA
      The squares under everything were wallpaper once: the same size and in
      the same place whatever the camera did, so they read as a texture on the
      glass rather than as the ground the work sits on — at 20% zoom a whole
      gizmo was the size of one square. The pattern is now a function of the
      camera. A cell is GRID_UNIT (20) WORLD units, so it scales with the
      zoom, and it is laid out from world 0,0, so it slides with the pan.

      lab.js writes three custom properties onto #bench and every skin's
      bench is cut from them: --gs (one cell, in screen pixels), --gs2 (five
      of them, for the heavier rule the map room and the armory draw) and
      --gx/--gy (where world 0,0 has got to). Nothing else has to know.

      A ladder keeps it usable at both ends: while a cell would land under
      GRID_MIN (10) screen pixels the step doubles, so far out you are looking
      at every 2nd, 4th or 16th line instead of a grey wash.

      Lab.grid(n) re-cuts the paper to a different cell. The dock's pixel tool
      is the one caller: choosing a pixel size rules the bench at that size,
      so the squares you can see are the squares you fill.

    STAYING SHARP WHEN YOU ZOOM IN
      will-change:transform hands the sheet to the compositor, which is what
      makes panning cheap — but a composited layer is rasterised ONCE and then
      stretched by the GPU. Left on permanently it means zooming in blows the
      old texture up instead of redrawing at the new scale: the paper, the ink
      and every gizmo went soft, and stayed soft until something else forced a
      repaint — which is why clicking a gizmo used to bring it into focus.

      So the promotion is a MOOD, not a setting. lab.js puts the hint on while
      the camera is moving (and on pointerdown, before it moves) and takes it
      off 140ms after the last camera frame, along with the 3d in the
      transform: translate3d(x,y,0) becomes translate(x,y). The two are the
      same matrix, so nothing shifts at the swap — the sheet is simply redrawn
      at the scale you landed on. Confirmed with the LayerTree: at 3× with the
      hint on, #bench-world is its own 984×8689 layer being scaled 3×; with it
      off there is no layer for it at all, so it paints at 3× and the drawings
      are drawn as vectors rather than blown up as pixels.

      Anything that keeps a layer of its own — the critters, the mayor, the
      gnome's sparkles — is animating every frame, so its hint is honest and
      is left alone. If one of those ever looks soft at high zoom, this is why.

    AND THE CANVASES  (Lab.hidpi)
      That only helps things the browser can redraw. A <canvas> is a bitmap:
      at 300% there are three screen pixels for every one the gizmo painted,
      and no amount of re-rasterising invents the other eight. Only whoever
      painted it can draw it again, bigger.

      So a gizmo hands its canvas over once:

        Lab.hidpi(canvas, () => draw())

      The canvas keeps the size it was written at as its LOGICAL size — the
      coordinates every drawing function already works in — and Lab fits the
      BACKING STORE to the pixels actually on screen (the canvas's displayed
      width, which for a width:100% canvas is not its attribute width, times
      the device pixel ratio), gives the 2d context a matching base transform
      so no drawing code changes, and calls redraw().

      Three things make it safe to hand over anything:
        · a canvas with no CSS size of its own would grow its gizmo when the
          bitmap grows, so hidpi probes for that once — widen the bitmap, see
          whether the box follows — and pins the CSS size if it does
        · only what is ON SCREEN gets the big bitmap (one IntersectionObserver
          on the bench); a gizmo parked off the sheet drops back to 1×, so a
          bench at 400% is not a bench full of sixteen-megapixel bitmaps
        · entries whose canvas has left the page are dropped on the next fit,
          so a gizmo may hand over a canvas from inside its own render loop
          (the dispensary does, for the trinket thumbnails)

      Refits wait for the zoom to SETTLE, and a ResizeObserver catches layout
      changes (a role switch, a narrower column) that the camera never sees.

      Handed over: both hill views and the third, the shed, the monument, the
      dig, the pen (which swaps its own logical size when it turns into the
      tank — hence hidpi.resize), the machine and its trinkets, the level
      meter, and the drawing pad.

      NOT handed over, deliberately: the forge screen and the critters, which
      are image-rendering:pixelated on purpose — hard pixels, sharp at any
      zoom, and a bigger bitmap would only smooth away the point of them.

      One thing this does NOT fix: pictures already saved as data-urls (the
      museum's frames, the capsule's drawings) are 128px bitmaps and there is
      nothing to redraw them from. The pad itself is now crisp to draw on, but
      what leaves it is still size×size, because the museum and the capsule
      keep those in localStorage and tripling them is a different decision.

  GIZMOS
    · drag by the handle bar
    · double-click a handle to send that gizmo back to its home position
    · press the – on a handle to put it back on the shelf
    · positions are saved per gizmo and survive reloads
    · gizmos can be dragged off the side of the sheet; the camera can always
      find them again

  MACHINES WITH NO HANDLE BAR
    Not every gizmo is a panel. The sticker-press machines (KN-0001, 0002,
    0010, 0014, 0015) have no case, no handle bar, no name plate and no feet:
    they are OBJECTS lying on the bench — a boiler, a barn, a monitor, a
    press — and an object has no window round it.

    So they are picked up by themselves. register() takes

        el.querySelector('[data-handle]') || el

    which means a gizmo that contains no handle bar IS its own handle: the
    drag, the double-click home and the – all hang off the machine. It also
    gets .gz-loose, which is only there to set the cursor.

    WHAT A DRAG MUST NOT START ON is one list, NODRAG at the top of lab.js:

        button, a, input, select, textarea, [data-nodrag]

    The first four are every control the lab has. data-nodrag is the opt-out
    for everything else that owns its own pointer or wants to be read rather
    than grabbed — a scrollable list, the chat log, the wanted poster, the
    forge's draggable needles, the ballot (which has its own drag), the help
    gnome's speech bubble. The same list guards the double-click home, or a
    double-click meant to flatten a warp slider would fling the whole machine
    back to where it started.

    The – still exists on these: it floats at the machine's top-left corner
    and only shows itself when the pointer is near. The name is not missing
    either — each of these designs prints its own name ON the machine, which
    is where a real one would have it. The serial stays in the markup,
    hidden, because the shelf reads it off .gz-serial.

  THE SHELF (left side panel)
    Lists every gizmo with its serial and a status dot. Click a live one to
    fly the camera to it; click a shelved one to bring it back out. The
    whole shelf folds away to a tab (it starts folded on small screens).

  ROLES — view the bench as somebody else
    Buttons top right, or keys 1 / 2 / 3:

      1  USER        what any visitor sees
      2  MODERATOR   review queues, strikes, take-downs
      3  OWNER       settings, the lines, the kill-switches

    A strip across the top of the bench says what the current role can do.
    Any element marked data-roles="moderator owner" simply disappears for
    other roles, so every gizmo can be inspected from all three seats
    without logging in as anyone.

  GENRE SKINS — what the place is dressed as
    Buttons next to VIEW AS, or shift+G to cycle. Knoll gives a community a
    hill, and a community is usually about something; for an indie game that
    something has a genre, and the place should not turn up dressed as a
    craft fair when the game is about a haunted basement.

      KNOLL       the house style — cream paper, aubergine ink, pink
      HORROR      the cellar — soot, bone type, one wound of red. A high
                    serif for names and a typewriter for the serials; grain
                    and a vignette over the whole bench; the name plates
                    flicker like a strip light on its way out
      ADVENTURE   the map room — parchment, oiled leather handles, brass
                    plates and brass rivets, engraved Roman caps. The dot
                    grid becomes a chart graticule and the sheet picks up
                    two soft stains
      SHOOTER     the armory — gunmetal, squared corners, hazard tape on
                    every grip, name plates lit amber, a fine technical
                    grid and a scanline over the bench

    HOW IT IS BUILT
      lab.css draws everything out of the token sheet in its :root — about
      eighty names covering paper, ink, the chassis, the plates, the rivets,
      the gnome. Nothing in lab.css knows a theme exists. themes.css re-points
      those same tokens under a [data-theme] on <html>, and adds a short
      second half per skin for what a token cannot say: the texture of the
      paper, the shape of a corner, whether the light flickers.

      Adding a skin is one block of tokens. Two things to know: --pink is THE
      accent and has to be legible as text AND take white type as a fill, so
      neons fail; and in a dark skin --ink (the type) goes pale while --ink-2
      (the chassis) goes darker than the paper, so a gizmo reads as a
      silhouette.

      The choice is applied inline in <head> before the first paint — put it
      any later and the page flashes cream on its way to being dark — and
      remembered in 'knoll-lab:theme'.

    THE ONE GAP
      Everything painted into a <canvas> — the hill, the shed, the monument
      yard, the dig, the bug farm, the vending machine — is drawn by JS in its
      own colours, and no token reaches inside a canvas. Rather than fork six
      painters, each skin GRADES them: one CSS filter over the finished frame,
      the way a film is graded. A blue sky goes cold, or goes to rot, but it
      is still the same sky. Teach a gizmo to listen for 'lab:theme' and
      repaint properly, and its selector comes off the grade list in
      themes.css. The drawing pad, the framed drawings and the arcade screen
      are deliberately never graded — you are making something on the pad and
      the ink must be the colour you picked.

  THE TOOL DOCK — drawing on the bench itself  (wall.js)
    The dock from the coming-soon hero, on the workbench: a dark pill at the
    bottom centre, and a second pill above it that carries whatever options
    the chosen tool has and is simply not there in Move mode.

      Move       drag anything already on the paper · reshape a note by the
                   tab on its right edge
      Draw       freehand ink · 4 widths (2.5 / 4.5 / 8 / 14)
                   · 4 pixel settings (off, 10, 20, 40)
      Sticker    stamp a shape · 5 stamps (pointy hat, gnome, toadstool,
                   big beard, lantern) · 3 opacities (100% / 55% / 25%)
      Text       leave a note · 5 fonts (Sans, Display, Serif, Mono, Marker)
                   · 5 stickies (none, yellow, pink, blue, green)
                   · a width, dragged, that the words wrap to
      Picture +  put a picture up: one off your own machine, or a gif
                   searched out of KLIPY. See below.
      5 colours  ink, pink, blue, green, orange — and any five you mix
      Undo       step back one piece      Clear  wipe the paper (it asks)
      Escape     shut the picture sheet, then put the pen down, back to Move

    THE + TOOL — a picture on the paper  (picture.js)
      The fifth thing on the dock. Press it and a sheet comes up over the
      bench with two ways in, and whatever you choose lands where you asked
      for it and then STAYS ON THE POINTER, so a second click on the paper
      puts up another. Click the bare paper with nothing on the pointer and
      the sheet opens remembering that spot, so what you pick lands there.

      IT KEEPS NO SECOND LIST OF PICTURES
        Pictures on this bench already have a home — THE ZOETROPE (KN-0013)
        holds them, drags them, dresses them up, takes them down, and gives
        the owner the size they land at and the switch that locks the drum.
        So the + tool owns none of that. It is a way IN: the sheet, the
        search, the key. Everything it finds is handed to Zoetrope.take or
        .takeRemote and becomes an ordinary pin. The drum's list shows it,
        the drum's × takes it down, its lock refuses it, and fx.js dresses
        it up like anything else made here.

      TWO KINDS OF PICTURE, KEPT TWO WAYS
        A file off this machine is read, shrunk and kept as a data-url,
        because there is nowhere to upload it to. A gif out of KLIPY is not
        copied here at all: what is kept is the URL they answered with, and
        the browser fetches the strip from them each time it is looked at.
        That is their terms as much as our storage — a provider's media is
        linked, never re-hosted — and it is why a 3MB strip that could never
        be kept costs about sixty characters. The drum's list says "from
        KLIPY" rather than a byte count for those, because saying "62 B" of a
        three-megabyte gif would be a true number about the wrong thing.

      WHY KLIPY, AND WHY IT WANTS A KEY
        Tenor's public API shut down in June 2026. GIPHY charges for a
        production key and its old public beta one (dc6zaTOxFJmzC) is banned
        — asked in August 2026 it answers 403 BANNED. KLIPY is ex-Tenor
        people with a near-identical API: free, 100 calls an hour on a test
        key and uncapped once you ask them for a production one. So there is
        no key to ship and none is pretended — paste your own into the sheet
        and it is kept in Lab.store('picture'), on this device.

        A key in a page with no server is readable by anyone using that
        browser. That is the honest place for a capped test key and the wrong
        place for anything else, and the sheet says exactly that.

        Their terms shape three things: the request goes straight from the
        visitor's own browser and is never proxied (KLIPY REQUIRE that, which
        is also why every call carries a per-window customer_id — a throwaway
        id, not a name, an email or a fingerprint); results are rendered in
        THE ORDER THEY ARRIVE with no filtering of our own; and their mark is
        on the sheet whenever their results are.

        The answer is unwrapped rather than assumed: providers move a list
        about between .data, .data.data and .result over the years, so the
        reader takes the first array of objects it recognises, and picks a
        rendition by walking sm/md/hd for a gif, webp or png — never an mp4,
        which an <img> will not play — with a last-resort walk of the object
        for anything that looks like a still. Being defensive about a shape
        we cannot test against is cheaper than being confidently wrong.

        ONE THING WORTH KNOWING: a cross-origin fetch that fails tells the
        page almost nothing — one opaque error whether the wire is down, the
        reply carried no CORS header, or the key was wrong. KLIPY's own "that
        key is invalid" comes back as a 404 with no CORS header at all, so a
        bad key lands exactly there. The sheet therefore says the key first
        and the connection second, rather than inventing a diagnosis.

      WHAT UNDO TAKES BACK
        Ink is kept in Lab.store('wall') and pictures in the drum's own
        store, so "the last thing put down" is not one list to pop. wall.js
        keeps a session-long note of which of the two each piece went into,
        in the order they were made, and undo reads it backwards — so a
        picture put up between two strokes is taken back between them too.
        It is not saved: after a reload undo pops ink, exactly as it always
        did, which is the right amount of memory for a button whose whole
        promise is "the thing you just did".

    THE PIXEL PEN — the grid is the picture
      Off, 10, 20 or 40. Squared off, a stroke is not a line but a set of
      CELLS: the pointer is floored to the grid and that square is added to
      one path, each cell only once however many times it is crossed, and a
      fast drag has the gap between two reported points walked so a quick
      line does not come out dotted. The whole drag is one piece, so undo
      takes the shape you drew rather than the last square of it.

      Choosing a size calls Lab.grid(), which rules the bench at exactly that
      cell — what you can see is what you will fill. The skins whose bench is
      dotted (the knoll, the cellar) rule themselves into lines while pixel
      mode is on, via body.wall-pixel; the two that already draw lines keep
      their own. Moving a piece afterwards snaps by whole cells, because
      pixel art nudged half a cell is no longer pixel art.

    A NOTE IS A BOX, NOT A LINE
      A note has a WIDTH, and the words fall onto as many lines as that width
      needs. SVG will not wrap text — a <text> runs off in a straight line for
      ever — so wall.js works the lines out itself and hands them over one
      <tspan> each, measuring on a canvas in the note's own face, weight and
      size (the face is a token, so it is resolved through the skin first).
      A word longer than the whole box overhangs rather than being chopped.

      You write into a box of that same width, in that same face and size,
      growing downwards as it wraps — so nothing rearranges itself when you
      press enter. ENTER pins it, SHIFT+ENTER breaks a line by hand, ESCAPE
      throws it away, and losing focus pins it too (which is why the grip
      has to preventDefault on the way down, or reshaping would file it).

      The tab on the right edge sets the width, while writing and again
      afterwards with the Move tool. Reshaping edits the item where it sits
      and repaints, and tells the store once on let go: a store write is a
      JSON round-trip of the whole wall, which is not a thing to do sixty
      times a second. Notes written before any of this hold no width and
      stay one line, as they were.

    Deliberately the same numbers as the hero, so the two feel like one thing.
    The stickies are the one exception: the hero has no such thing, and a note
    written before they existed simply has none, which is what index 0 is.

    THE STICKIES — something to write on that is not the paper
      Under the fonts, a second group of five: none, and four pads — yellow,
      pink, blue, green. Pick one and the note is written on it. (These stood
      in for five scrolls until August 2026 — a scrap, a roll, a banner, a
      sealed charter. Same slot, same index 0, same cut-to-fit machinery; a
      note written on scroll 2 is now on the pink pad.)

      Each one is CUT TO FIT. Nothing is a fixed picture: the words go down
      first, are measured with getComputedTextLength(), and the pad is then
      drawn around whatever width they came out — so 'x' gets a little square
      and a full line gets a long one, with the same margins on both. Since a
      note can wrap, stickyArt() takes a HEIGHT as well: the block is as wide
      as its longest line and as tall as it has lines, and the pad is cut to
      that. Left out, the height is one line's worth, which is what every
      caller wanted before notes could wrap.
      paint() reads every width first and writes every sticky after, which is
      one layout pass for the lot rather than one per note.

      What sells it as paper rather than a rectangle: the bottom-right corner
      is CUT AWAY and that same triangle drawn back over the diagonal in the
      underside colour, which is a fold; a band of glue across the top, where
      it is on the real thing; and a shadow of the whole shape offset a couple
      of units, so it lies ON the bench rather than being printed into it. The
      fold is kept in proportion, so a one-word sticky is not mostly fold.

      None of them is TILTED, and that is deliberate rather than an oversight:
      dragging a piece sets style.transform on it, a CSS transform beats a
      transform attribute, and the words are drawn straight in world units by
      the caller. A rotated pad would need the words rotated with it and would
      leap to the corner the moment you picked it up.

      Because the width is measured rather than stored, it has to be measured
      again whenever the words could have moved: once when the web fonts land
      (the first paint runs on fallback metrics) and again on every 'lab:theme'
      (a genre swaps --display and --mono for a different pair). Both are wired
      to paint at the bottom of wall.js.

      The pad is --sticky-a…d and the fold --sticky-edge, so it follows the
      genre like every other colour here — a pad has to be a paper the SKIN's
      own ink reads on, which means proper lemon and rose under the knoll and
      something dim and dirty down in the cellar, or a pale ink would vanish
      into it. stickyArt() also draws the dock's own little previews, at note
      size, so a button is a sample of the thing rather than a symbol for it.

      That leaves one problem the scrolls did not have: four nearly-black pads
      on a near-black pill is a colour picker you cannot see the colours in.
      So on the DOCK ONLY, lab.css (.opt-sc) pins the four to fixed washes
      that keep their hue and read on the dark — the artwork is untouched,
      only the tokens under it are swapped, the same trick the stamps use.

    WHAT IT DRAWS ON
      One <svg> inside #bench-world, in world units, so ink pans and zooms
      with the paper and stays crisp at 400% instead of going to porridge the
      way a bitmap would. Everything on it is one of four plain records — a
      stroke, a squared-off stroke, a stamp, a note — and that is also exactly
      what is saved, so the wall is a list of primitives, not an image.
      Kept in Lab.store('wall').

      HOW FAR THE INK REACHES
        The layer is the size of the world, and the world used to be a sheet
        with a visible edge, so ink stopping at that edge made sense. The
        paper has no edge any more, and an invisible one you cannot draw past
        would be worse than the box that was taken away — so the layer carries
        a transparent square far bigger than the world. It is PAINTED, which
        is what makes it answer a pointer, and the svg is overflow:visible,
        which is what lets it be rendered outside its own box. It is not a
        .wall-item, so in Move mode it goes as inert as the rest of the layer
        and clicks fall through to the paper underneath.

    COLOUR FOLLOWS THE PAPER
      The hero keeps two palettes and swaps to the darker one after dark,
      because ink that suits cream paper disappears on a night sky. The bench
      has that problem four times over, one per genre skin, so a colour is
      stored as a TOKEN NAME and resolved when it is painted. Draw in horror,
      switch to adventure, and the ink follows the paper rather than vanishing
      into it. The five are --ink, --pink-2, --blue, --green, --amber, which
      are the hero's five day colours exactly.

      … UNLESS YOU MIX YOUR OWN
        The five swatches are pots, not fixtures. Press the one already
        chosen and the system colour picker opens on it, loaded with the
        colour that swatch is ACTUALLY painted — read off it a moment before
        the dialog opens, so mixing in the cellar starts from the ink the
        cellar uses. Alt-click puts the house colour back.

        An ink is therefore one of two things, and a saved mark can hold
        either: a NUMBER, which is an index into the five and so re-mixes
        itself with the skin, or a HEX STRING, which is meant to stay exactly
        that colour wherever it is looked at. Everything drawn before the
        picker existed holds a number, so nothing had to be migrated, and a
        mix applies to NEW marks only — what is already on the paper keeps
        the colour it was made with. The mixes live in the wall's own store,
        beside the drawing.

        The picker itself is ONE <input type="color"> shared by all five,
        parked under the swatches: it cannot live inside a <button>, and five
        of them would be five more tab stops for something a click already
        opens.

    THREE THINGS THAT WILL BREAK IT IF FORGOTTEN
      · '.wall-ink' has to stay in lab.js's onPaper and HANDS_OFF lists, next
        to .critter and .zt-pin, or a drag on bare paper pans the camera
        instead of leaving a line. The layer also drops its pointer events
        whenever the hand tool is up, so panning always beats drawing.
      · shutNote() lets go of its reference BEFORE removing the input, because
        removing a focused element fires blur synchronously and blur is what
        commits a note. The other way round, escape behaves like enter and
        silently keeps the note you just cancelled.
      · a sticky is drawn in WORLD coordinates, not inside a translated <g>.
        Dragging a piece sets style.transform on it, and a CSS transform beats
        a transform ATTRIBUTE — give the group one and the whole pad leaps to
        the top-left corner the moment it is picked up. It is also why no pad
        is tilted: the tilt would have to go on that same attribute.

  THE TWO RESET BUTTONS (top right)
    reset layout   puts every gizmo home and re-centres the camera
    reset data     wipes everything saved on this device — petitions,
                   drawings, ballots, blocks, the lot. It asks first.

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  3.2  Shared parts every gizmo can use
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  THE HILL  (hill.js)
    One hill, drawn on as many canvases as want it. Gizmos register a source
    of items; every attached canvas shows the union of all of them. So the
    pond the petition board digs is the same pond the naming ceremony names,
    the same pond the shed ships next to, and the same hill the time capsule
    is buried in.

      item kinds: pond, tree, flag, bench, sign, stone, contraption
      things born in the last ~1.5s grow in with a little bounce
      hover any item and its label appears in the caption under the canvas
      named things answer with their name first, then what they are
      while someone holds the office of mayor, the lawn gnome wears the sash

  THE DRAWING PAD  (pad.js)
    A pocket sketching surface any gizmo can bolt on — a small square canvas,
    five pots of ink, a rubber and a wipe. Used by the museum and the time
    capsule. Hands back a PNG data-url on request. Always white paper, never
    transparent. Stays on the device.

  CRITTERS  (critters.js)
    Whatever the forge stamps out is dropped loose onto the bench and wanders
    around: amble somewhere, stop, look about, amble somewhere else. Four
    pre-rendered sprite frames per creature. They startle and scurry if you
    swipe the cursor at them, but hold still if you rest on one — then you
    can pick it up and carry it. Drop one in a registered bin and it is gone.
    Nine at most, or the bench becomes a zoo.

  THE LITTLE NOTE  (note.js)
    A speech bubble that hangs off a point on the bench. Gizmos use it when
    the thing you clicked is painted into a canvas and so cannot hold a panel
    of its own — a bug crawling round the farm, a trinket in the dispensary's
    case. Screen space, so it stays legible at any zoom, and it follows the
    thing while the camera moves under it. One open at a time; Escape, a click
    outside, or opening another closes it.

      Note.open({ at: () => ({x, y}), html, cls, on: {sel: fn}, onShut })

    The anchor is a FUNCTION on purpose — whatever it points at is usually
    moving. Return null from it and the note closes itself.

  DRESSING THINGS UP  (fx.js)
    Click anything somebody made — a creature loose on the bench, a drawing on
    an easel or hung on the wall, a keepsake out of the time capsule — and a
    small card offers five animations and five effects. Hover one to try it on
    the real thing; click to keep it. One of each at a time, so they stack.

      ANIMATE   bob      floats gently up and down
                hop      little springy jumps, squashes on the landing
                wobble   rocks side to side, like it might go over
                shimmer  the hand-drawn boil — stepped, so it snaps between
                         drawings instead of sliding
                spin     turns all the way round, edge-on halfway

      EFFECTS   lantern  a warm halo that never sits quite still
                ghost    see-through, softly blurred, slowly breathing
                neon     a sign-tube glow in pink and blue, bad contact
                         included
                sticker  a white die-cut edge, lifted off the page
                stone    grey, cool and chiselled

    Wiring a gizmo in is one attribute: data-fx="<a stable key>" on the
    element that should be clickable, which must have one child holding the
    picture. The outer element is the STAGE and carries the animation (a
    transform, so it must not have one of its own); the first child is the
    SKIN and carries the effect (a filter). Two elements, because both an
    animation and an animated effect want the `animation` property and one
    element only has the one.

    The card lives in screen space, not bench space — it stays the same size
    whatever the camera is zoomed to, and follows its creation while the
    canvas moves under it. Re-render a list as often as you like; a
    MutationObserver re-dresses whatever comes back. Creature costumes are
    dropped at boot, since the creature will not be there either.

  THE CAUTION TAPE  (tape.js)
    Strips of hazard tape you string across the bench to cordon off whatever
    is not finished. A prop, not a gizmo: no panel, no serial, no place on the
    shelf. Add one from the TAPE button on the dock.

    IT SPANS TWO POINTS, not a width. The design it came from only stretched
    left and right, which is fine on a page and useless on a bench where the
    thing you want to cordon off is diagonally across from you — so each end
    is a handle you drag, and the length AND the angle fall out of where the
    two ends are. Drag the printed middle to move the whole strip.

    It is pinned to the SHEET, not the screen, so it stays over the gizmo it
    is about while the camera pans and zooms. Hover a strip for its ✕.

  THE HELP GNOME  (guide.js)
    A baffled-looking gnome stands in the corner of every gizmo. He does not
    know how the thing works either, but he has read the label — click him
    and he explains the gizmo in a speech bubble: what it is for, the steps,
    who can do what, and a closing tip. One bubble open at a time; Escape, a
    click outside, or another gnome closes it.

      The machine reads the pixels: a seed (a hash of the picture), the main
      hue, how busy it is (chaos) and how bright (lumen). Drag a needle to
      overrule a reading, double-click it to hand it back, "auto" to hand

      with it how many colours the palette is allowed and how hard they are

  03 WARP BENCH

  Tip: clean, flat backdrops cut out best. Busy photos come out as confetti —

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  № KN-0012   THE SOUND DESK  MK.I                                        │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
The six tracks the coming-soon page pretends to play, actually playing.
THERE IS NOT AN AUDIO FILE ANYWHERE IN THIS PROJECT — every note is generated
with Web Audio and seeded off the track name, so a track sounds like itself
every time without a byte being shipped.

  STAFF ONLY. The panel is gated to moderator and owner. A visitor gets the
  small speaker and slider in the zoom dock, and nothing else — the sound of
  a place is not something every visitor should be able to redesign.

  01 The desk      six knobs, four switches, and a VU needle reading the
                   analyser, not faking it. Drag a knob up and down; double-
                   click to reset; tab to it and use the arrow keys.
  The knobs        LEVEL · BASS (lowshelf 220Hz) · TREBLE (highshelf 2.8k) ·
                   TONE (lowpass, 300Hz–15.6k) · REVERB (wet/dry) ·
                   SPEED (0.5×–2×, tempo only)
  The switches     TAPE   pitch follows the speed knob, the way tape does
                   CRUNCH a tanh waveshaper, driven until the edges go
                   WOBBLE an LFO on the gain — a slow, unsure tremolo
                   HALL   a much longer generated impulse for the reverb
  02 The playlist  the same six, with artist and running time. Click one to
                   go there; the little bars mark what is playing.

  THE CHAIN
      voices → crunch → bass → treble → tone → wobble ─┬→ dry ──→ level
                                                       └→ hall → level → out
    The reverb impulse is generated too — decaying noise, 1.1s normally and
    3.6s with HALL on. Nothing is fetched.

  HOW A TRACK IS MADE
    Each track carries a tonic in Hz, a scale in semitones, a tempo and a
    mood. A seeded LCG over a hash of the TITLE lays out 48 or 64 steps of
    scale degrees with plenty of rests, and that pattern loops. Same title,
    same tune, every load. Wheelbarrow Waltz is in three, which is why its
    pattern is 48 steps and its accent falls on beat four of six.

  NOTHING STARTS ON ITS OWN. Browsers refuse audio until somebody presses
  something. The desk restores every knob from the store but never begins by
  itself, and the dock speaker doubles as the start button for a visitor.

  moderator  the desk, the switches, the playlist
  owner      the same
  user       the speaker in the dock: press to start or mute, slider for how
             loud. It drives the very same LEVEL knob.


┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  № KN-0013   THE ZOETROPE                                                 │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
A zoetrope is the Victorian drum that spins a strip of stills until they move.
This one takes YOUR OWN picture — off your machine, not off the internet — and
tacks it to the paper, where it can be dragged about, dressed up with the usual
fx card, and taken down again. A gif turns. A still sits there.

  01 THE DRUM
      Three ways in, all the same road: "choose a picture", a file dropped on
      the drum, or paste. png, jpeg, gif, webp, avif, svg.

      Dropped on the BARE PAPER it lands exactly where it was let go; any other
      way it lands in the middle of whatever the camera is looking at. A file
      dropped on a gizmo is left to that gizmo — the forge has an intake of its
      own and has to keep it — and a paste into a field being typed in is left
      alone too.

  02 ON THE PAPER
      Everything up, newest first, with what each one weighs. "find" flies the
      camera to one and flashes it; "take down" unpins it. You get your own; a
      moderator gets everyone's.

  03 THE WORKS  (owner)
      How big pictures land (130 / 190 / 280 across), whether the drum is open
      to visitors, how much is being kept on this device, and "clear the paper".

  WHAT HAPPENS TO A PICTURE  — nothing leaves the device
      It is read with FileReader, shrunk here, and kept as a data-url in
      Lab.store('zoetrope'). There is no server to upload it to and none is
      pretended.

        · a STILL is redrawn down to 900px on its long side and re-encoded as
          webp (png where webp cannot be written, so transparency survives) —
          a 4.5MB png measured at 28KB going in, and identical at pin size
        · a GIF is kept byte for byte. Putting one through a canvas keeps frame
          one and throws the rest of the strip away, so instead of quietly
          ruining it the drum refuses anything over 900KB and says why
        · an SVG is kept as it is: small already, and the one kind that stays
          sharp however far the camera goes in

  THE ROOM IS MEASURED, NOT ASSUMED
      localStorage is a few megabytes for the WHOLE bench, and Lab.store
      swallows a write that fails. So before a picture is promised, the drum
      RESERVES the characters it needs under a scratch key and hands them
      straight back. Without that, a picture would pin, look fine, and be gone
      on the next reload — the worst of both, since you would have believed it.
      With storage deliberately filled to the brim, the drum says there is no
      room and pins nothing.

  THE SEARCH CAME BACK, SOMEWHERE ELSE
      This gizmo used to crank a word at a gif provider itself, and for a
      while that half was gone entirely. It is back — but as the + on the TOOL
      DOCK (picture.js, documented in 3.1), not as a panel here. The searching,
      the key and the sheet of results live there; what it finds comes back
      through takeRemote() and is pinned like anything else. So this file still
      talks to nobody and keeps no key. It only knows that a picture may be a
      link rather than a copy.

      The provider notes, since that list churns and finding it out again is
      the expensive part: Tenor's public API shut down on 30 June 2026 and
      stopped issuing keys that January. GIPHY charges for a production key,
      and the free beta key everyone used (dc6zaTOxFJmzC) is now banned — it
      answered 403 BANNED in August 2026. KLIPY — ex-Tenor people, near-
      identical API — is free: 100/hour on a test key, uncapped once you ask
      for production, attribution required, and it REQUIRES that requests come
      from the end user's browser rather than through a proxy, which suits a
      page with no server exactly.

      A FOURTH WAY IN, and what it changed here
      take() now takes an ECHO as well, so every message it makes is repeated
      to whoever asked — the + tool has a line of its own to report on, and
      this gizmo may well be on the shelf while it is used. take() and pin()
      also hand the pin BACK, because the + tool needs to know which picture
      it just put up: which one to take down if undo is pressed, and which one
      is still on the pointer.

  user       puts pictures up, drags them, takes down their own
  moderator  takes down anybody's
  owner      the size they land at, the switch, the broom

      A LINKED PICTURE WEIGHS A URL
      takeRemote() runs the same gates as take() — the lock, the count, the
      room, the pin — and refuses anything that is not on https. What it does
      NOT do is read, shrink or cap, because there is nothing here to shrink:
      sixty characters of URL is the whole of what this device keeps. The size
      comes from the rendition the provider quoted, and is only measured off
      the picture if they did not say.


┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  № KN-0014   THE CHATTER-TRON 2000                                       │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
A big putty-coloured monitor from about 1987 sitting on the bench, with a
board running on it that everybody looking at the page can type into, and
sticky notes taped down the side of the case that only staff may write on.

  "EVERYBODY ON THE PAGE" IS NOT A FIGURE OF SPEECH HERE
      There is no server behind this bench and none is pretended anywhere
      else in the lab, so a chat board is the one gizmo that would have to
      fake it. This one does not. Two WINDOWS of the page open on this
      machine are two people, and they really do talk to each other:

        storage           the board itself. localStorage fires a 'storage'
                          event in every OTHER window the moment one of them
                          writes, so a line typed here is read back and
                          redrawn there. That is the whole sync, and it is
                          real. (Lab.store holds its copy in memory and would
                          otherwise go on believing it, which is why the new
                          value is read back over the top rather than merged.)
        BroadcastChannel  who is on. Each window shouts a short hello every
                          four seconds and answers anybody else's; one not
                          heard from in fifteen seconds drops off the list,
                          and one closed properly says so on the way out. It
                          also carries a nudge when somebody speaks, so the
                          board updates on the instant rather than on the
                          next event.

      Open the lab in a second window and watch the list grow and the power
      lamp light. What it will NOT do is reach another machine — that needs a
      server, and the panel says so rather than implying otherwise.

  A WINDOW IS A PERSON, A DEVICE IS NOT
      Every other gizmo keeps its identity in Lab.store, which is per DEVICE.
      That is right for a drawing and wrong here: two windows would be one
      person talking to themselves. So the handle lives in sessionStorage,
      which is per WINDOW — a second window is somebody else, and a reload is
      still you. You get a name off the shelf (mossy gnome, kettle crow) and
      can type over it; the badge beside it shows which role you are wearing
      while you speak.

  01 THE SET
      The monitor: a deep hood with the glass sunk into it, vents across the
      case, a neck, and the slab of a keyboard under it that you actually
      type the board into. The last 120 lines are kept and the oldest scroll
      off the top. A board that is at the bottom stays at the bottom when a
      line lands, and stays put if you have scrolled up to read something.
      The case is --crt-shell, which the skins re-point, so it is putty on
      the knoll and grim in the cellar rather than beige everywhere and wrong
      three times; the glass is the same --screen-deep and the same scanlines
      the forge's screen uses, because they are the same make of machine.

  THE STICKIES DOWN THE SIDE
      Three notes taped to the case, the way they are on every monitor that
      has ever been in an office. They are the board's standing text — the
      rules, a notice, whatever the place needs saying — so a visitor may
      READ them and nothing else. A moderator writes on them and cycles a
      note's colour; the owner may also add and take them away, up to five.

      A visitor gets the same box, readonly, rather than a different one:
      nothing shifts when the role changes, and it is plainly not theirs to
      type in. They are the dock's own four pads (--sticky-a…d and the glue
      strip), so the bench has one idea of what a sticky note looks like.

      A note is written where it sits and told to the store on the way OUT of
      the box — a store write is a trip to localStorage, a redraw of every
      note and a shout at every other window, which is not a thing to do per
      keystroke, and a redraw mid-sentence would take the caret with it. The
      event is 'focusout', not 'blur': blur does not bubble, and delegating
      from the list means catching it on an ancestor.

  02 WHO'S ON
      Your handle, and everybody the channel has heard from. You are marked,
      and a moderator or owner wears their badge here as well as on the board.

  03 THE WORKS  (owner)
      The board can be locked — moderators and the owner may still type, and
      everybody can still read. Add a note to the case, or wipe the board (it
      asks). Nothing said here leaves the browser.

  user       reads the notes, types on the board, takes back their own lines
  moderator  writes the notes and their colours, takes back anybody's line
  owner      adds and removes notes, locks the board, wipes it

  THE SAME BOARD, A DIFFERENT SET. The monitor was restyled into the sticker
  press: a wide putty case, a blue screen, and the notes moved to the LEFT of
  the case. A line is a SPEECH BUBBLE now rather than a ledger row — yours
  cream and on the right, everybody else's yellow and on the left, which is
  the one thing the old row could not say at a glance. The sync underneath is
  untouched; crt.js gained two lines, for the "N ONLINE" readout in the bezel.

The bench's own history, printed the old way. Nothing here is written by

  HOW AN ISSUE HAPPENS
      Nobody presses anything, and there is no PRINT button — the news is not
      a thing you make, it is a thing that has already happened. Everything on
      the bench is stamped with a date, the events are grouped into the DAYS
      they fell on, and every day that had news is one issue. The newest sits
      on the press; the rest go on the newsstand, newest first.

      A petition carried or repealed, a thing shipped out of the shed, a mayor
      elected, a drawing varnished, a landmark named, a bug swatted. Each is
      ranked, and the loudest that day takes the headline, the next two get
      columns, and the rest go in the PATCH NOTES at the bottom. RANK, at the
      top of press.js, is the whole editorial policy; nothing else reads it.

  READING ONE
      Click any paper. It lands folded and the bottom half drops open on a
      hinge. Escape, the ✕, or a click outside folds it away.

  ISSUE No. 001
      Always "THE PRESS IS RUNNING", dated the day before the oldest thing on
      the bench, so the stand is never empty and the numbering has somewhere
      to start.

  THE WAX  (owner)
      The letter in the seal. It is stamped on every paper on the stand, so
      changing it re-stamps the lot.

  IT READS, IT NEVER WRITES. Every neighbour is reached for defensively and
  all of them are optional — a lab with the museum shelved still prints a
  paper, it just has less in it. press.js must load after the gizmos it
  reads. Its own state is one letter of wax.


────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  3.4  How it all joins up
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The gizmos are not sixteen separate demos. They are wired into each other,
which is the actual thesis of the product:

    petition carries  ─→  something is dug into THE HILL
                      ─→  an Article appears in THE LAW BOOK, by itself

    a new landmark    ─→  THE NAMING CEREMONY opens a ballot on it
                      ─→  the name it wins shows on every hill on the bench

    the shed ships    ─→  the thing is screwed to THE HILL
                      ─→  a ✦ line lands in THE LAW BOOK

    a mayor is voted  ─→  the sashed gnome wanders the whole bench
                      ─→  the lawn gnome on every hill wears the sash

    the forge stamps  ─→  the creature walks loose on the bench until
      out a creature       somebody bins it

    anything a person ─→  can be clicked and dressed up: bob, hop, wobble,
      made                 shimmer, spin, and lantern, ghost, neon,
                           sticker, stone

    the dock's + puts   ─→  it is a pin in THE ZOETROPE'S list, with the
      a picture up          drum's lock, its ×, its size and its dressing-up

    a second window     ─→  is a second person on THE TERMINAL, and the two
      of the page           boards really do talk

    a bug is agreed   ─→  it grows in THE BUG FARM tank, and at fifteen
      with                 me-toos it cracks the glass

    a capsule is      ─→  the trinket lands in THE DISPENSARY cabinet, where
      twisted open         it can be dressed up like anything else made here

    the coming-soon   ─→  are the six THE SOUND DESK actually plays, down to
      page's six           the running times printed on the card
      invented tracks

    a ballot is       ─→  the vote is the SAME vote the old buttons cast, so
      posted into          the hill is dug and THE LAW BOOK is written while
      THE BALLOT-O-TRON    the tally screen is still printing

    anything at all   ─→  turns up in tomorrow's KNOLL TIMES. The press reads
      happening            the petitions, the shed, the elections, the museum,
                           the naming ceremony and the bug farm, sorts it into
                           days, and sets the front page itself. Pass a
                           petition and the paper on the press has already
                           changed.

Pass a petition in one panel and watch three other panels react without
being touched. That is the demo.

    Serve the site folder over http (file:// will trip some of it up):

        npx serve C:/Users/bobb9/Desktop/site

    then open  /          for the coming-soon page
               /lab/      for the workbench

  STORAGE KEYS
    wallspace-wall-v4 ......... the hero wall's drawing
    ws-last-art, ws-last-game . so the demo doesn't repeat itself
    knoll-lab:cam ............. camera position and zoom
    knoll-lab:pos:<gizmo> ..... where each gizmo sits
    knoll-lab:shelved ......... which gizmos are on the shelf
    knoll-lab:shelf-folded .... whether the shelf is folded away
    knoll-lab:role ............ user / moderator / owner
    knoll-lab:fx .............. what is dressed in what
    knoll-lab:sound ........... every knob, switch and which track
    knoll-lab:<gizmo> ......... each gizmo's own saved state
    knoll-lab:ballot .......... how many ballots this device has posted
    knoll-lab:press ........... the letter in the wax
    knoll-lab:tape ............ where every strip of caution tape is strung
    knoll-lab:bugfarm-poster .. where the WANTED poster was left

    "reset data" in the lab clears the gizmo states. Clearing site data in
    the browser clears the lot, including the hero wall.

  THINGS TO BE CAREFUL WITH
    · The coming-soon page is a Design Canvas document. Editing index.html
      by hand fights the tool — make structural changes in Design Canvas.
    · The site auto-deploys to Vercel on push. The lab is untracked and
      does not ship; keep it that way unless that changes on purpose.
    · Nothing in either page has a backend except the signup form. Every
      vote, drawing, block, ballot, bug and trinket is per-device and
      vanishes with the browser's storage. That is fine for a prototype
      and NOT fine for the real thing — all sixteen gizmos assume a
      shared server later.
    · The bug farm's evidence photos are the one thing on the bench that can
      eat real storage: three jpegs per bug, in localStorage. They are boxed
      down hard, but a farm full of screenshots will hit the browser's few
      megabytes before anything else does.
    · The lab now runs two art directions side by side on purpose. lab.css is
      the original — cream paper, aubergine ink, pink, and every gizmo in a
      case with a handle bar on it. sticker.css is the press: cream, one hard
      black line, one hard shadow, Kalam and VT323, and NO case at all —
      those five are objects on the bench rather than panels. KN-0001, 0002,
      0010, 0014 and 0015 are the press; the other eleven are not, and the
      bench looks like two shops until the rest are brought across. That is a
      decision, not an oversight.
    · Because the press machines have no case, the space around them inside
      their section box is still theirs — an invisible margin that drags the
      machine rather than panning the bench. It is a generous grab area, but
      if a machine ever feels like it starts too far out, that is why.
